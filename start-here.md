## Disclaimer: Buyer Beware

Let me get this said as soon as I can: I make zero claims of being someone you want to listen to without criticism (unlike Claude, Shelby, Olley, Milliken & Milliken). One of the best pieces of advice I’ve ever heard is:

> Only take advice from people that you want to become like.
> 

Why do I bring this up? Because after all my time and effort into my years as a student participant in FSAE, our team finished in a glorious 58th out of 70 (a placement that I’ve never been more proud of). We received the “Team in most need of improvement” award. I tripped and fell on my face on my way up to the stage to accept the award. I wish I was kidding. 

I fully recognize that there are MUCH more qualified individuals to write on these topics. In fact, my advice to you is to read them - having achieved success in this endeavor, they are qualified (whereas I am not) to actually lay out the details on how to get to the top. 

This collection, however, is not primarily aimed at an experienced audience. I want to make more ‘onboarding material’ for the newcomer. For the people who are on the outside looking in. 

This is my best effort at compiling what I wish I had when starting and running an FSAE team. In my searching so far (limited as it is), much fewer of the top teams and leaders get their thoughts down to paper than I’d like. And most of the time, their advice, writing, and focus is on understanding the higher tiers of the game. This book is not that. This book is the “starter kit”. This book is the best hypothesis that I’ve been able to come up with on how to get into the game. If you follow the advice herein, you will not win competitions. You will not become famous for your innovative designs. You will not inspire looks of awe from your competitors. But, importantly, you’ll be in the game. 

That is, in my opinion, the greatest, most underutilized part of the incredible program that is Formula Student: the amount of people that it involves. The program is too valuable for so few to be influenced by it. Formula and programs like it (Baja, rocketry, mars rover, etc), are what I believe to be what universities are uniquely positioned to offer students that no AI can ever replicate: team-based, long-term, immensely challenging endeavors focused on achieving an objective. I believe that we need more players in the game, and this series is my effort to help them get in. 

## Reasons to Trust

Now, having stated a proper “Buyer beware”, here’s some reasons why you *should* listen to me:

Within Formula Student:

- I founded the BYU formula team from ZERO prior knowledge.
- We had very little professor/university involvement. We were entirely student driven.
- Our program’s yearly participation peaked at 210 students, with 150 Weekly Active Members. That year, our lowest was of 60 students, 40 Weekly Active Members.
- We passed tech on our first competition appearance.
- Our program has placed candidates at:
    - Tesla
    - Lucid
    - Ford
    - Toyota
    - Rivian
    - Bridgestone
    - Red Bull F1

Outside of Formula Student:

- I worked as a data engineer for a Porsche Racing team.
- I currently work at Rivian as an Electrical Systems engineer.

In general, I’ve been in love with automotive engineering my whole life. While I don’t know everything (not even close to it…), I have had deep experiences so far that are valuable to pass on.

### A Love Letter

This is my love letter to FSAE - my attempt at contributing to the sport I love and spent years learning about. 

In all things, I need more data to back up my claims - use my arguments as a starting point, but start even earlier with the mindset to ask why and challenge everything. By doing so, you’ll dig out and pour your own foundations, which is much more valuable than copy and pasting my beliefs into your head. 

While I’m passionate and driven to contribute value to the Formula Student on-ramp, I ask that you start with the understanding that everything that I say deserves to be challenged. I make many efforts in my writing to provide a baseline to start with - things like expectations, a template/beginning car design, and team structures. I make the argument for these concepts because you need to start somewhere in order to start. And that is what I want over everything else: for you to start. 

That said, my baseline concepts are not to be treated as ‘copy and paste’ beliefs. One of the most effective parts about Formula Student is that is forces you to *think on your own as an engineer.* It makes you to weigh your options and understand them. It causes incredible growth for you as an individual because of that. So keep that in mind as you read through - my arguments are not to be blindly accepted. They are to be challenged. 

### The Original “Claude Code”

A favorite quote of mine from an Formula Student legend, Claude Rouelle, is:

> If you know WHY you are winning, you can win again and again.
> 

That is my goal: get you to where you know WHY the winners win, and start you on the path. As I’ve said before, the “starter kit” will not produce a top result. But it will produce a result. And the best part is: it will also produce the start of an amazing new Formula Student program. 

To get to the exciting and complex engineering of active aero on a carbon monocoque (which is a fancy lightweight frame), you MUST start with a simple, straightforward car. None, I repeat, NONE of the currently winning teams got there without starting simple. It is the only way to sustain team growth and success across a long period of time. 

Claude’s code also says: At first, focus on building an A level team and a C level car. If you try to flip them around, it is *extremely* unlikely that the car will truly be A level. Why? Because the knowledge and experience it takes to make an A level car is hard fought, and hard won across years of effort. If you try to skip to that, it is highly probable that the new team will not be able to achieve it. If you try, fail, then disband, then your long-term results are guaranteed to be zero. 

Instead, follow the wisdom of those that have gone before you. Focus your energy on building the team that builds the car. If you form your team in a sustaining, healthy way, then the car’s complexity and performance will naturally rise as the years go on.

## What this collection is

This collection is meant to serve as a primer for what you need to know to get started. When getting started myself, I was frustrated at how difficult it was to find good information grouped together by system. The too-expensive but fantastic “Learn & Compete” was and is necessary reading, especially because the chapters therein are written by actual subject matter experts and experienced professionals, unlike this book. But (and its a big but) its subjects are only barely sequenced and not very cohesive, which is likely due to the fact that each chapter is written by a new author. Therefore, the chapters don’t really flow together. That said, I will pull a great many working principles and examples from that text, as it served as a big part of my foundation to Formula SAE. 

This series is an attempt to travel through every aspect of FSAE at a level that is understandable to the absolute beginner. As I am closer to the beginner side than the expert side, I hope that my tone and style prove to be easy reading to one who finds themselves with a lot of enthusiasm and no experience (as I had when I started). I’ll cover what I believe to be the most important fundamentals in this wonderful endeavor, although to truly compete “at the pointy end” of the competition, you’ll need wisdom and advice and understanding from more experienced sources than me. This is meant to be the “Getting Started” guide, the “read this first” book that should set you up with a good starter kit for getting into the game. That said, perhaps the deepest lesson that I’ve learned in my time is that FSAE guarantees challenges that are far outside of your reach, so there will undoubtedly be holes and missing pieces. Won’t stop me from trying anyway (a useful attitude when starting an FSAE team).

Through a series of 10 books (and an extra story), I’ll lay out as much as I can about each aspect that I believe makes up the foundation for a solid FSAE program. Here is the list and a description of the main ideas of each book:

This foundation will set the frame for all of the other volumes, so I suggest starting here.

They say that “Repetition is the mother of learning”. You will see many of the same things explained to you over and over again throughout this series. This is by design - our minds need reps in order for things to stick. If you are already familiar with the series, then you’ll be able to skip over sections without any loss in value. I try to structure each chapter in this way:

- The introduction to the chapter gives a simple, easy-to-digest version of what the chapter is about. This is the ‘first rep’. The introduction sections should have no math and be like a story of how the system works. Diagrams only. A picture book.
- The main part of each chapter will go over the concepts in deeper detail, with some math used where needed. This is meant to be your second time through the concept.
- The end of the chapter will summarize the key takeaways so you can reference them later to remind yourself what was in the chapter without having to read it again.
- I also include links and relevant references to other Formula Student content, so you can go even deeper. This section is my effort at aggregating the resources that I found useful for each subject.

## The Template Car

There is a LOT of information. Upon diving in, you may immediately be disappointed to find that I do not spend enough time on how to achieve the most performant carbon-fiber layups for your active aero setup (in fact, I won’t spend any time at all on that outside of this sentence). That is because there is enough information to learn contained within the basics already without bothering with the competitive end of Formula Student. It is my mandate that you adopt the absolute minimum viable racecar for the first years. Its what I will refer to as the “Template Car”. A starter car. The simplest car that still passes tech. The ‘bone stock’ version of an FSAE car. This series is for the beginners, and so that is the car that we will be discussing. As previously stated, progressing beyond that will require deeper devotion and study from the true greats of the sport - the UTA’s, the Michigan's, the ETS’s. But believe me when I tell you: there will be more than enough to learn by designing and racing the literal minimum-acceptable entry that still gets out on track. 

The Template Car, defined:

- Space frame chassis
- Single motor
- Chain driven
- Hoosier Tires
- Motorcycle brakes
- Basic Accumulator
- No Aero
- Direct, double A-arm suspension

If you don’t know what any of that means, that is perfectly ok. That is what this series is for! We’ll go step by step through each of those systems and how they work.

## Drivers, Start Your Engines (before we dive in)

After reading a fair amount of racing literature, I’ve mostly seen that it’s written by experienced racers, for experienced racers. My efforts will not be aimed at the experienced racers. My goal is to make the reading exciting and digestible for the absolute beginner - no preexisting knowledge required. I try to keep my references to famous racing moments in history to a minimum, as those tend to be much more of a “if you know, you know” test. I will also focus every concept and reference into the Formula Student context. This means that some things I argue for in this series may be foolhardy to attempt in other racing series. I just always found myself trying to fit the racing references and discussions from other books with an Formula Student filter with middling success. Here, I’ll start with the assumption that we’ll see everything in the Formula Student frame, only departing from it to other series to make a strong point. 

Basically: everyone writes for people who are already in the know, and no one writes about Formula Student specifically. I’m trying to fix that. 

It is my view that we don’t need more literature about how to excel - there are teams that excel very well every year. What we don’t have is a healthy mid-to-low end of the field - a paltry 30% of entrants make it out on track. We need that number to get higher. The top cats competing for the podium will always be here - what we need is more racing for the majority. You’ll see - in the end if you got 5th or 55th it won’t matter as much as if you got to see your creation get pushed around by overworked college kids, or if you got to witness your homemade rocket take off and carve corners. 

There is no easy way to do it. There is still going to be a mountain of work. I hope this book takes you 1% closer, but make no mistake, you will still have to do the other 99%.

An analogy I liked: I can help get you in the row boat. I can help you point in the right direction. I can sit beside you and cheer. But to get to the other side, you have to row. And in Formula Student, there is a LOT of rowing. 

Now let’s get started.

Welcome to the game.
