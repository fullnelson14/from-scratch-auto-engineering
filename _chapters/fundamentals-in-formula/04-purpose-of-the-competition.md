*This part covers Sections GR, AD, and PS in the Rulebook.*

The very first rule of the book starts with the most important point: the reason the competition exists. Let’s read it verbatim:

> 
> 
> 
> Collegiate Design Series
> SAE International's Collegiate Design Series (CDS) programs prepare undergraduate and
> graduate engineering students in a variety of disciplines for future employment in mobilityrelated industries by challenging them with a real world, engineering application.
> Through the Engineering Design Process, experiences may include but are not limited to:
> • Project management, budgeting, communication, and resource management skills
> • Team collaboration
> • Applying industry rules and regulations
> • Design, build, and test the performance of a real vehicle
> • Interact and compete with other students from around the globe
> • Develop and prepare technical documentation
> Students also gain valuable exposure to and engagement with industry professionals to
> enhance 21st century learning skills, to build their own network and help prepare them for the
> workforce after graduation.
> 

*Rule GR 1.1, page 5*

All of the sections in the first part of the rulebook are there to set down general requirements and definitions of the series as a whole. Things like what documents you need to submit in order to be eligible for the competition, how to resolve rule conflicts or protests, and how to register for competition. All of it needs to be defined, but it isn’t exactly the exciting part about the rulebook. What’s unfortunate is that all this boring stuff is right at the beginning. This means that many well-intentioned students set out to read the rulebook, but are often stopped by the dry definitions in the beginning. My advice? Skip these sections for now. They only become important for team leadership, and they are more like checkboxes than anything else. Here’s the best highlights:

### Engineering Competition

Rule GR.1.3 states:

> 
> 
> 
> Formula SAE is an engineering education competition that requires performance
> demonstration of vehicles in a series of events, off track and on track against the clock.
> Each competition gives teams the chance to demonstrate their creativity and engineering
> skills in comparison to teams from other universities around the world.
> 

Emphasis on engineering education competition. All of the effort and coordination and structure of the Formula Student Program is to focus on making you a better engineer. To give you an education in engineering. 

### Student-Based Teams

Rule GR.9.1.1 states:

> 
> 
> 
> Vehicles entered into Formula SAE competitions must be conceived, designed, fabricated and
> maintained by the student team members without direct involvement from professional
> engineers, automotive engineers, racers, machinists or related professionals.
> 

This backs up the purpose of the competition. I think this is more to maintain the effort and focus of the competition on student growth than it is to prevent unfair advantage in the form of industry enthusiasts giving a certain team a leg up. As you’ll see, it is very challenging to build a program that is capable of finishing in the top 5. Even if you had Max Verstappen (an extremely skilled driver currently driving in F1) as your driver, you are still not likely to outperform the many schools that put up reliable results every year. It is, after all, an engineering competition more than it is a racing competition.

### Only First Year Vehicles Allowed at Competition

There’s technically *some* flexibility here, but the main point is: plan on bringing a new chassis to competition every year. Rule GR.9.3.1 states: “Only First Year Vehicles may enter the Formula SAE Competitions”. A few lines earlier, in Rule GR.9.2.2, a First Year Vehicle is defined as: “A vehicle which has, at minimum, a newly built chassis and is in its initial Competition Year”. 

This means that every time you go to the yearly competition, you’ll need a new chassis. There can be many reused components year to year (like the motor, the wheels, etc), but the frame itself must be newly built specifically for that year. This is for many reasons, but one that I most understood is: when dealing with completely custom vehicles, it is difficult to assess the long-term safety of the frame. Let’s use the example of a welded steel frame. Most of the time, the frame gets painted before it shows up at competition. This often hides details about the quality of the welds. The inspectors can ensure that the right bars are welded in the right places, but it is much harder to tell how long a certain weld would last without removing paint. For that reason, it’s much easier to say that all teams must bring a new frame each year instead of trying to assess the health of each and every frame from over 80 schools.
