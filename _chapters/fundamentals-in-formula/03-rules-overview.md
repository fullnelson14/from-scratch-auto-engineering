The first law of any FSAE writing or reading is that there cannot be any FSAE writing or reading without the required discussion about the rules. The rules dictate everything that is allowed within the competition, and are the entry ticket into earning points. Many newcomers to FSAE bemoan the rules as dry, boring things that other people should know, not them. I echo the statements of the wise: you MUST read and understand the rules. They are the foundation for EVERYTHING in FSAE and they are the gate by which you score all points. In fact, they define where you get points from, and even suggest what events are worth focusing on over others (if you look hard enough). If you are stubbornly thinking to yourself “yes, that’s great, but I’m a special snowflake and I don’t need to read the rules because I just want to design, the others can take care of the rules”, you might wake up to the fact that there is no design *anywhere* without regulations. In any industry, field, product line, or anything that an engineer ever designs, there will be applicable regulations that they must design within. Welcome to the real world. 

You might take heart in the fact that, as far as regulations go, the Formula Student rules are some of the thinnest regulations that you’ll ever come across. In fact,  I’ll make the claim that the Formula Student program has the least amount of rules in all of auto racing. There is a staggering amount of freedom within the rules. They are intentionally designed that way. The entire purpose of Formula Student is to help you stretch and grow as an engineer, and the organizers have reflected that within the rules. Want to make active aero? Go for it. Want to create a 6-wheeled monster? Be my guest. After reading them the first time, you will see that a grand majority of the rules are there simply to ensure safe operation for the driver and other participants. After that, its open season. Think of it like this: Formula Student will be the least regulated product you will ever develop. As you get into industry, you will quickly find that every product type has a large amount of regulations that apply to it. So instead of seeing the rules as a killjoy, choose to see them for what they are: the minimum set of rules for balance and safety that the organizers can operate with. 

Rules and regulations also have a special role to play in racing: they are the map to discover what you can exploit. A book called *How to Build A Racecar* by the legendary Formula 1 engineer Adrian Newey illustrates well the principle that in Racing, very often the unfair advantages that allow your car to perform better than competitors is due to a eagle-eyed reading of the rules. 

One of the biggest evidences of that is the fact that there is no minimum weight. If you find a way to get the driver around the tracks with only 50 kilos of weight, you can do that. Minimum weights are very common in most of motorsport, so not having one is an exciting engineering challenge. 

Even though I have demanded that you read the rules, I’ll make things easy on you by introducing some key rules within this chapter, so you can get an easier intro to them instead of just facing them on your own. 

There are 12 sections in the rules, each with their own emphasis. One of them is focused on the specific rules that apply to cars powered by an engine, and one only applies to cars powered with a battery. That means for any given team, there are 11 sections in the rules to fully understand before achieving a fully legal vehicle. 

Most Formula literature will mandate that you need to read and understand the entire rulebook in order to get going. This can have a paralyzing effect on new participants - there are many terms and regulations that are quite unfamiliar to your regular, everyday language and much of the content can be pretty dry. Things get especially boring when you try reading about systems that you have no interest in. 

The tricky part about the advice to read all of the rules is: the advice is correct. In order to fully control your design and steer it into a version that fits within regulation, you’ll need to understand most/all rules. Even the rules that seem like they don’t touch your system will probably have an effect on how you design things. For that reason, the advice to fully understand all rules is sound. 

However, there is an important distinction here. For more experienced members and leaders of the team, reading and understanding the entire rulebook is a non-negotiable - after all, the responsibility of if the car passes tech or not lies with the chief engineer and team leads, so they need to know what the rules are. But for the new perticipant, I recommend skipping straight to the good parts of the rules, and leveling up your depth in the rules as your responsibility on the car grows. 

To make things even easier, I’ll take you through a guided tour of the exciting parts of the 2026 rulebook. This will help you get your footing on the major highlights of the vehicles, help you ‘speak the language’ of the series with other team members, and give you a great start with understanding what this series is all about.

We’ll walk through the rulebook in 5 parts, where I’ll highlight the most impactful rules in each part. The parts will be:

1. Purpose of the Series/Competition
2. Vehicle Rules
3. EV-Specific Rules
4. Tech Inspeciton
5. Competition Details

After reading through this tour, you won’t know everything, but you’ll know the most important parts.
