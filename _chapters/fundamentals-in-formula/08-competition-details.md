Outside of Technical Inspection (covered in the previous section), there are 8 events in total at the competition. Three of them are “static” events, meaning the car does not need to move during it, and you can participate in them without having passed tech inspection. The other 5 events are called “dynamic” events, and they all require driving. You’ll need to pass tech in order to participate in these. 

All of the 8 events are opportunities to earn points. The dynamic events are the events that earn the most points, as they are the hardest to finish and do well in. Let’s discuss the basics of each event to give you an idea of what goes on at competition. There are a total of 1000 points possible spread across all of the events. Let’s break down each one. 

### Static Event 1: Presentation Event

> 
> 
> 
> **Presentation Event Objective**
> The Presentation Event evaluates the team’s ability to develop and deliver a comprehensive
> business, logistical, production, or technical case that will convince outside interests to invest
> in the team’s concept
> 

*Rule S.2.1*

You might wonder why something like a Presentation Event is included in the competition. After all, isn’t this an “engineering competition”? Why are we assigning points for this “business”-style event? 

The answer is: because there is no engineering without effective communication. Either you have already seen or will see soon that all forms of engineering require some way to communicate what you have done to people that may not understand the same details that you do. Particularly when it comes to financial decisions surrounding engineering, you’ll need to find ways to link your product and its benefits to how it impacts the business or endeavor that you are a part of. This is a deeply important skill as an engineer, as it contributes to how easy it is to interface your work with the work of others. Therefore, it is wise for the organizers to include a challenge such as this, as it ensures that the students envolved think more holistically about engineering, instead of getting stuck into “make-car-go-fast” mode.

Don’t get me wrong, “make-car-go-fast” mode is *very* important (as reflected by the amount of points that you’ll earn by making a fast car), but being able to connect your engineered product to a real business case is so fundamental to engineering that it couldn’t be left out. 

The Presentation Concept gets posted on the SAE Event website a few months before the competition. It will include a prompt and a scenario for which each team must prepare a business presentation for, using their team’s car design as their main focus. As an example, here is the 2026 Presentation Concept:

![2026 Presentation Concept]({{ "/assets/images/fundamentals-in-formula/presentation-concept-2026.png" | relative_url }})

As you can see, the Presentation Concept contributes very well to your success as an engineer and as a team - this one in particular helps you get skills for approaching sponsors! We’ll dive into sponsorships in [*Execute in Formula*]({{ "/books/execute-in-formula/" | relative_url }}), but you can trust me when I say: securing sponsorships has a *huge* impact over the progress of your team. 

You can earn a maximum of 75 points in the Presentation Event. 

### Static Event 2: Cost and Manufacturing Event

> 
> 
> 
> **Cost Event Objective**
> The Cost and Manufacturing Event evaluates the ability of the team to consider budget and
> incorporate production considerations for production and efficiency
> 
> *Making tradeoff decisions between content and cost based on the performance of each part
> and assembly and accounting for each part and process to meet a budget is part of Project
> Management*
> 

*Rule S.3.1*

Exactly how matching your engineering skills up against a business case helps you see the whole of engineering, matching your design to what it costs to make will accelerate your abilities as an engineer. In the Cost and Manufacturing event, you are tasked with recording the manufacturing processes and costs of your entire car. 

When starting out in engineering, it can be very easy to get tunnel vision on a design. When this happens, you tend to start favoring certain solutions or parts, usually because you were the one to come up with it, or you saw an F1 team doing it, or you think “I’ve never seen anyone else’s part look like this…. it must be so good that no one has discovered it yet”. Allow me to put an end to the daydream: someone has thought of it before. Paraphrasing one of my favorite quotes from SuperfastMatt’s FSAE book *Racecar*: “Very often students want to ‘think outside of the box’ without first getting to know what is in the box. In the case of FSAE, its a *really* big box.”

The cost and manufacturing event helps stretch your understanding of design and engineering by looking at your chosen design in a new light. The cost event will ask you things like:

- What was the raw material you started with?
- How much did that cost?
- What manufacturing operations did you have to do to it?
- How many times?
- How many fasteners (i.e. nuts and bolts) are on it?
- What tooling was required for the manufacturing?
- How long does it take to manufacture?
- How long did R&D take for the part?

As you can see, this gets really in-depth for each system. You will quickly start to understand your own choices in a new light. Sure, making some suspension A-arm parts out of carbon fiber can end up lighter than steel, but chances are, after all the material costs and manufacturing costs and manufacturing time, you might be better off with just the steel ones. 

Thinking like this will help you see deeper detail into why other products are the way that they are. When I was a young boy, I remember thinking “why does Honda make their armrests out of this cheap plastic material? It makes the car feel so low-quality”. I did not have much experience making hundreds of thousands of Civics then. I still don’t, but I at least understand that every decision in engineering has multiple competing priorities, and looking into the cost of materials and manufacturing has been one of the best sources of learning why. In the case of Honda Civics, there is not likely to be many other armrest material types that can be made in such high quantities for so low a price. 

As far as the Cost Event goes, you’ll be asked to put together a Cost Report for your entire car that takes the above questions into account. You’ll also be asked about a Cost Scenario while at the competition, which is a hypothetical challenge related to the cost or manufacturing of the vehicle. You can earn a maximum of 100 points in the cost event.

### Static Event 3: The Design Event

> 
> 
> 
> **Design Event Objective**
> The Design Event evaluates the engineering effort that went into the vehicle and how the
> engineering meets the intent of the market in terms of vehicle performance and overall value.
> 
> The team and vehicle that illustrate the best use of engineering to meet the design goals, a
> cost effective high performance vehicle, and the best understanding of the design by the team members will win the Design Event.
> 
> Components and systems that are incorporated into the design as finished items are not
> evaluated as a student designed unit, but are assessed on the team’s selection and application
> of that unit.
> 

*Rule S.4.1*

This is probably the most well known static event, as it is the easiest to include in descriptions about the competition. The Design Event is a time set apart during the competition when you will bring your vehicle, in the most finished condition possible (ready to run) to a tent where a judging team awaits. The judging team is made of 5-6 industry professionals who will examine your team’s car and the design decisions made in it. They will evaluate the engineering effort that went into your car and judge how well your car achieves the objectives set out in the rules. They will also investigate the team members’ knowledge about the systems that they designed to judge how deeply each designer understands their system. 

As part of the Design Event, you’ll be required to provide engineering documentation about your vehicle, including a Design Spec Sheet, which records key information about your vehicle like its overall weight and key features. You’ll also be required to submit 3-View Drawings, which are engineering drawings of your overall vehicle in a top, side, and front view.

Like the other two static events, this event helps you round out your skills and understanding of engineering. Being able to defend your design choices and provide engineering documents for the specification of your design are key skills. This event earns the most points out of all the static events (150), so it is worth preparing well for. It is common for teams to bring posterboards, binders, testing data, or infographics to support their design decisions during judging. This section is just an intro to the event; a deeper discussion on strategy for this event and others will be in [*Compete in Formula*]({{ "/books/compete-in-formula/" | relative_url }}).

### Dynamic Events

Now on to the fun parts. The dynamic events and the exciting displays of agility and power are why most of us get hooked. I know that my first time seeing a quad-motor Formula Student vehicle tear up an endurance track changed something in me - “is it really possible to build something like that from scratch?”

In this part of the rules there are a few sections about the general regulations surrounding the flag signals that the track marshals will use to communicate with drivers as they operate their vehicles. There is also the rules that pertain to the drivers from each team. There is a “Three Event Limit” for every driver (*Rule D.7.1)* , meaning that each individual driver can only drive for 3 of the events. This means that each team will end up needing 4 total drivers on their team, as the Efficiency event counts towards the drivers’ event limits. Each event has multiple tries available per team. 

There are 5 dynamic events at the competition, with the Efficiency Event being combined with the Endurance Event (as in, they happen at the same time). Let’s walk through each one. 

### Dynamic Event 1: Acceleration

This event is straightforward. Literally - just make the car go straight forward as fast as you can. The official track is just a straight section of pavement for 75 meters. You’ll set up and put the car in ready to drive mode at the start line. Then, when the marshals tell you that the course is clear, you are free to drag race your way from zero to 75 meters. The times that your car is able to achieve, as measured by some laser gates set up, will be the metric that will get scored against the other teams. 

You get 4 tries in total, two per driver. We’ll talk more strategy in [*Compete in Formula*]({{ "/books/compete-in-formula/" | relative_url }}), but the basics for this event are to make your car/driver combo the lightest possible. There is no race driving skill needed for this event - just nail the pedal down to the max and hope for the best. 

The Acceleration Event is worth 100 points to the fastest team. This is a good time to explain that in the dynamic event, all of the teams are scored against each other, meaning that the team with the lowest Acceleration time will get 100 points, and everyone else will get a lower score that is based off of how far away their time is from the fastest team’s time. All of the dynamic events are like this. 

### Dynamic Event 2: Skid Pad

This event is meant to measure how well your car can corner. “Skid Pad” is the automotive industry term for a big flat area of pavement where you drive cars around in a circle to test how fast they can go. For the Formula Student competition, the Skid Pad course looks like this:

![Skid Pad course layout]({{ "/assets/images/fundamentals-in-formula/skid-pad-course.png" | relative_url }})

This diagram comes straight out of the rulebook. The car will enter the course on the bottom and do two laps around the right circle, then switch to the left circle and do two more laps around it, then exit the course. During this event, you are trying to go as fast as you can without spinning out or hitting any of the cones (represented as squares on the diagram). The cars that are good at turning/cornering will have lower times in a course like this. 

It is important to highlight that the rules completely define the shape of this course, so it is possible to set one up in a parking lot wherever you are. In fact, doing just that is a VERY good idea - competition happens in a frenzy of just a few days, so any familiarity that you already have is really helpful for saving time. 

Just like Acceleration, you get two runs per driver for two drivers. This is a total of 4 tries to set your fastest time. Hitting cones is not an automatic disqualify, but it will add hefty time penalties. 

The Skid Pad event offers 75 points to the fastest team and lower to everyone else. 

### Dynamic Event 3: Autocross

Now things get interesting. The Autocross event is a point-to-point course set up on flat ground with a lot of cones. There is no laps, just a race against the clock through a tricky course. Like the other events, hitting cones will add big time penalties. Unlike the previous two events, the organizers do not share the exact track definition beforehand - the first time your driver sees it at competition will be the first time that anyone has seen it. 

Even though they are custom-built every year, there are some building blocks of the course that will always be included. Things like a slalom section, where you have to weave in-and-out of cones, is basically guaranteed to happen, as well as tight hairpin turns. The building blocks that they use are defined in *Rule D.11.1,* which serves as a great reference for building your own autocross tracks in your parking lot to test in. 

The driving tries are the same as before: two drivers get two tries each. Fastest time through the course over those four tries is your team’s time. Because your driver hasn’t seen this track specifically, this event will test their driving fundamentals - how well can they handle the car on a course that they have not driven before? This event and Endurance are where having trained drivers will really make a big difference in your overall times.

Fastest time gets 125 points.

### Dynamic Event 4: Endurance

The big one. This is the crowning achievement of a Formula Student team. It is the most fun to watch and the most difficult to finish. It is the hardest to do well in, as reflected by the points possible (278). Over 25% of your team’s possible points comes from this one event. 

Like the Autocross event, you won’t know the track beforehand, but the rules give you some basic building blocks to make a representative course. There will be straights, hairpin turns, slaloms, and constant-radius turns. The Endurance event is the first event where there will be multiple cars on the track at the same time. Each car has a specified start time and the objective is still to race against the clock, there is simply too many competitors to only have one car on track at a time. To accomodate multiple vehicles, there are a few passing zones built into the track, which are a straight section of track that has two lanes: one for the slower car to pull into and let the faster car pass, and one for the faster car to stay in and speed ahead. Track marshals with blue flags will indicate to the slower driver that they are required to let the car behind them pass.

There is only one attempt allowed for the Endurance race. It is a track designed on a flat surface with cones, just like the Autocross event, except that this time you’ll be driving the same track lap after lap. The full distance that you’ll be required to drive is about 22 km spread over the many laps. A driver change is required during this event, so you’ll need two of your drivers to participate. Basically, your car will race with the first driver to start the event, drive approximately half of the distance, enter the driver change area, swap with the next driver, then finish out the rest of the distance. This driver change is not a pit stop - you have a set amount of time allowed to make the change. You get 3 minutes from the moment that your first driver stops in the Driver change area and shuts off the car. You can finish your driver change before that and get going, but 3 minutes are set aside for the driver change regardless and do not count against your time. If you go past that time, you will start adding time to your overall Endurance race time. 

As stated before, this event is hard to complete. The full race amounts to about 40-45 minutes of driving, about 20 minutes per driver. Ideally, you are driving hard for every second that the car is on track. Driving hard for extended periods of time puts the most amount of stress on the car (which is exactly what this event is trying to test). After driving hard for a while, the tyres get hotter, the batteries get hotter, the driver uses lots of energy, the brakes get hotter - basically everything gets pushed to its limit. Many teams have not progressed far enough with the reliability of their car, and so will try to drive their car in a more gentle way through the Endurance event. Breaking down in the middle of the event is a very real possibility, especially when you have never driven your car for that long before (which is when breaking down is almost guaranteed). 

Because the points possible are so high for this event, it ends up driving a lot of your car’s design decisions like how big the battery should be, what type of tyres you should use, what brakes and cooling system you should use, etc. There are 275 points possible in the Endurance event. This event is so point-heavy that teams need to try everything they can in order to make their car/drivers participate in it. As we’ll cover, the Efficiency event happens at the same time as the Endurance event, meaning there are even *more* points possible in this event.

### Dynamic Event 5: Efficiency

This event evaluates the energy that your car used to complete the Endurance event. Because the Endurance event is so long, it gives a good picture of how efficiently your car converts stored energy into distance travelled. By rule, each car is equipped with an Energy Meter, which measures how much energy the car is using at any time and records data as you drive. After the event, the recorded data is taken off of the Energy Meter and converted into your efficiency score by using some factors described in the rules. Basically, they measure the total amount of energy used, divide the distance travelled, and convert it to a common “CO2 score”, which is then compared against other teams. 

For EVs, you have the opportunity to design and use “Regenerative Braking”, which means that it is possible to charge your batteries when the car is braking. This is advanced capability for vehicles and it requires a lot more effort to get working correctly. The teams that succeed in doing it reap the benefits in the Efficiency event. There are 100 points possible in the Efficiency event.

Let’s look at the overall spread of points possible in the competition:

![Competition points by event]({{ "/assets/images/fundamentals-in-formula/competition-points-by-event.svg" | relative_url }})

This shows clearly: it is worth giving more attention to the things that give the most points (i.e. Endurance/Efficiency. Grouped together from lowest points possible to highest:

![Competition points sorted low to high]({{ "/assets/images/fundamentals-in-formula/competition-points-sorted.svg" | relative_url }})

I grouped Endurance and Efficiency together because they happen at the same time. That means that 37.5% of your possible points come from the Endurance event. If you combine that with the Design event, those two events account for 52.5% of your points. 

The whole point here is that these point allocations should be the foundation for driving your design decisions. When you come up against early design choices like: How many battery cells do we use? Should we include Aero wings on the car? Should we make a push-rod suspension?

The answers to those questions should start with a followup question: Which solution path gives us the highest chances of success in Endurance and Design? Use the point allocations to guide your thinking and discussions.

Put another way: if you participate and finish every event, *even if you are the slowest ones*, it is very hard to do poorly.

![2025 competition results — 29th place and below]({{ "/assets/images/fundamentals-in-formula/competition-results-2025-29th.png" | relative_url }})

This is a strong indicator that most/all of the teams below 29th did not pass technical inspection. That is over half of the competition, which registered 80 teams total. TLDR: it does not matter how fast your car is or how complicated your design is if it cannot go out on track and earn points. You should build your foundations of Formula Student on the basic order of:

1. Make the car pass tech
2. Make the car finish all the events
3. Make the car do well in Endurance
4. Make the car do well in every other point-earning event

If your team does not pass one of the previous milestones, you need to simplify your plans and designs and go back to the basics before moving on to the next milestone. 

## Overall Rules

There you have it. A quick primer on the entire rulebook. You’ll need to dive into the actual rulebook in order to get the specifics for your design, but this gives you a great foundation from which to build, as well as a good idea of what happens at competition.
