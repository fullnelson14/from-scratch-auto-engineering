Let’s start this section with a direct quote straight from the rules about Tech Inspection: 

> 
> 
> 
> The objective of Technical Inspection is to determine if the vehicle meets the Formula SAE
> Rules requirements and restrictions and if, considered as a whole, it satisfies the intent of the
> Rules.
> 

This means: the rules are there to ensure safe and fair execution of a Formula Student racecar. And Tech Inspection is there to make sure that your car fits within the rules. 

Tech Inspection is a long series of checks and tests that SAE organizers perform on your car in order to evaluate if your car passes the rules. What this means in practice: you spend about 2 days pushing your car through stations where inspectors wait with their specific rule checks. At each station, 2 or 3 inspectors will check your car to see if it has the required features according to the 10-12 rules that they are checking. This also involves taking of certain parts of your car in order to show them in more detail, as well as answering questions about your car should anything be unclear. 

Tech Inspection for Formula Student can seem like a tough challenge to get through, and you might wonder why the organizers place such a high priority on it. I like to compare it with something like Spec Racing, where each of the cars in the competition are built to the same specifications. Before Spec Races, there is a tech inspection that is MUCH faster, where the organizers just need to ensure that the car is set up normally before giving it the pass to go out on track - the car is built exactly like all of the other cars that it will race against, so as long as its got the key safety components, its good to go. 

Now contrast that against the Formula Student Tech Inspection. In Formula Student, ALL of the cars are built from scratch, so the organizers have no way of knowing what the car is built out of or how it was put together. Formula Student’s first purpose is to serve as an Engineering competition, and to help each participant grow and stretch as an engineer. This means that a *huge* variety of ideas and designs are possible within the rules - because that’s exactly what the competition is all about!

This means that there is no easy way to ensure the safety of the cars without a deep inspection into exactly how each team put them together. So that is exactly what happens at competition. 

The good news is, there is absolutely zero ambiguity about what will be checked on your car. The rules lay out every requirement that your car must have as well as every test that will be performed on your car. In addition, SAE publishes beforehand the exact same inspection forms that the organizers will use at the competition, so you can (and should) run “mock tech inspections” to see how your car stacks up. SAE gives you every tool necessary so that there are no big surprises when it comes to competition. 

Once at competition and during the days of Tech Inspection, it is very common to receive notes from the inspectors about areas where your car is lacking. At that point, you usually pull your car out of the inspection line and return to your team’s pit location, where you can work to bring the car into being rules legal. The goal that you need to hit while at competition is: pass technical inspection with enough time remaining at competition to compete in all of the events. This means that the fewer ‘re-do’ items that you must do to your car, the better, as it will save you precious time. As you compete for the first few years, don’t be surprised when the inspectors find things about your car that need to be fixed before you pass. The main objective of using the tech inspection worksheets in the weeks leading up to competition is to ensure that no major items will be missed.

There are 7 stages within Tech Inspection: 

1. Initial Inspection / Gear Check
    1. This is where your driver equipment will be checked (things like gloves, helmets, suits) to make sure they are valid. Fireproof racing gear (the type that is required) have certification dates and expiration dates, and your gear must be within its expiration date. 
    2. SAE also checks some other gear like Fire Extinguishers and Wet Tires for your car. 
2. Tractive Battery Pack and Charger Inspection
    1. Your Tractive Battery must be kept separate from your car when you get to competition (i.e. it cannot be installed into the car). This is so SAE inspectors can look inside the Tractive Battery to make sure everything has been done right. Only after the rigorous Tractive Battery inspection will you be able to install it into your car.
    2. This inspection also includes checking your battery charger.
3. Mechanical / Electrical Inspection
    1. This is the main inspection that everything besides the battery will undergo. There are many stations within this stage, and the inspectors will check basically every physical part on your car. 
    2. In particular, the frame structure will be inspected closely to ensure that your car is crash-worthy. Each rule of the “F - Chassis and Structural” section will be closely examined. 
4. EV Active Inspection
    1. If your vehicle passes both Tractive Battery and Mechanical Inspections, then you’ll be approved to install the battery into your car. At this point, your car must pass some vehicle-level checks, like the shutdown circuit tests that we mentioned before. Vehicle-level test means something that the car must do when it is all the way put together - everything besides the driver. 
5. Tilt Check
    1. For this test, your car must have everything on it/in it as if it was going to drive, including your tallest driver. Your vehicle is placed on a special machine that tilts it sideways. The car must be able to tilt at 60 degrees to the horizonal without rolling over, which is the equivalent of 1.7 g’s of sideways acceleration (if you don’t understand what that means, that’s ok. It basically means that the tilt test makes sure that your car is really hard to roll over during driving).

    ![Tilt test]({{ "/assets/images/fundamentals-in-formula/tilt-test.jpg" | relative_url }})
    
6. Rain Test
    1. This test makes sure that your car doesn’t become dangerous in the event of rain. During the competitions that I have been a part of, there has been no rain. That is not a guarantee however, and SAE must ensure that all cars are safe to operate in the case that the weather starts raining. For this test, your car is put into drive mode, where the High Voltage energy is active. Water is then sprayed onto your car for 2 minutes straight. Then, your car will sit for another 2 minutes without anyone touching it. If your car’s shutdown circuit activates during this time, you fail, as your car is not waterproof to rain. If your car stays in drive mode with no issues, then you pass.

    ![Rain test]({{ "/assets/images/fundamentals-in-formula/rain-test.webp" | relative_url }})
    
7. Brake Test
    1. We mentioned this test before in the Brakes section above. In this test, you must drive your car in a straight line for a certain distace and then demonstrate the ability to lock up all four wheels. This proves that your brakes are capable of stopping the car. If you can pass this final active test, your car is approved to participate in the dynamic events. 

That’s it! If you pass through those 7 stages, you can go earn points in the dynamic events (which we will discuss soon). 

For each stage of the tech inspection, your car will receive a sticker that identifies it as passing that stage, like a passport. 

As I’ve mentioned many times, you’ll need a more in depth understanding of Tech Inspection in order to pass it, but our objective here is to just get you a base layer to build upon. Now you’ve got one. 

## Tech

It is also impossible to have a discussion about the competition in Formula without discussing the immense challenge that is technical inspection. Formula Student teams are tasked with building a custom, from the ground up, from scratch, race car every year, which means that, for the most part, every team is bringing a completely new vehicle to the competition every year. You may not have ever thought of Formula race cars in this way before, but they are basically a human surrounded by a cage of metal with four wheels and enough energy in the battery to represent a small explosive. On top of that, race cars are only race cars because they travel around tracks at very high speeds.

All this being the case, and probably the reason why you are interested in the sport anyway, is also the reason why there is a massive focus on safety over the four days that the competition takes place. Three of those four days (and, for most teams, all four days) are taken up with inspecting each team's car to guarantee that they are able to be operated in a safe way, both for the driver and for other competitors and spectators and track workers standing by the track. 

 Because of the dangers and risks present with strapping a young student into a bomb cage on wheels that's able to achieve high speeds, the inspectors who are tasked with ensuring that each car is safe will do an excellent job of critiquing each car that comes into technical inspection according to the regulations that SAE sets forth.

The good news is that SAE makes these regulations available for all teams to be able to know beforehand. In fact, SAE makes the exact forms that the judges use to evaluate the safety of each vehicle available for all teams to be able to use as if they were judges themselves. In a perfect world, you would be able to hold a mock tech inspection with your car and perfectly be able to determine if your car is worthy of entering the track and competing in the dynamic events. Technical inspection is only required for competition in the dynamic events, whereas participation in the static events can be done by a vehicle that does not yet pass technical inspection. 

Technical inspection within Formula Student is an extremely difficult task and test to pass because, as you can imagine, there are many rules pertaining to the safety of the participants. So many rules that, for the newcomer, it is difficult to fully incorporate all of the rules on the first time that you are able to build a car. Put differently, it is extremely difficult for new teams to:

1. learn enough about how to design a race car and the tools to actually do the design

2. learn enough about how to allocate the right materials, get the right resources, purchase right materials, and form them into a race car

3. organize enough people to be able to do tasks one and two and four

4. organize the right schedules to travel to the competition

5. do all of that while understanding the rules and regulations sufficiently enough such that the car that you built passes

 The good news is that getting to know the rules is just like any other skill. It can be learned, and it can be done. This book will help guide you through what I believe to be the best way to learn the rules. As you spend time with the rules and regulations, they will make more and more sense to you, and you will pick up more and more on the terminology and intent behind the rules.
