### Vehicle Style

Rule V.1 (the first one of the section): 

> The vehicle must be open wheeled and open cockpit (a formula style body) with four wheels
that are not in a straight line.
> 

Simple enough, but gives you an official definition from the rules on what we are building. The next rule defines what “open wheel” means, here’s the diagram:

### Vehicle Size (In general)

Rule V.2.1 states:

> 
> 
> 
> The vehicle must be able to accommodate drivers of sizes ranging from 5th percentile female
> up to 95th percentile male.
> 
> - Accommodation includes driver position, driver controls, and driver equipment
> - Anthropometric data may be found on the Event Website

This means that your car needs to be able to fit someone large *and* someone small. There are ways to do this well, which we will get to. The important part to understand now is that the car you design needs to take a range of sizes into account; you are not designing the car for one specific driver. 

### Necessary Suspension

Rule V.3.1.1 states:

> 
> 
> 
> The vehicle must have a fully operational suspension system with shock absorbers, front and
> rear, with usable minimum wheel travel of 50 mm, with a driver seated
> 

What’s important here to point out is that the car must have a suspension that “travels”, or goes up and down in an amount of at least 50 mm, or about 2 inches. How you accomplish that is up to the designers, but you must have one. There are many racing vehicles that do not have any suspensions (see pictures) and gain some advantage because of that tradeoff. But Formula Student cars must have suspensions.

### Frame Rules

The entirety of the F - Chassis and Structural section of the rules (which is about 21 pages) deals with the specific rules that your frame needs to pass in order to be considered safe. This is the main “crash cell” that protects the driver in the case of an accident, so getting the details right is really important. These rules deal with things like where specific bars need to be on your chassis and how to protect your car’s battery. Here’s an example of the type of rules:

![Chassis triangulation diagram]({{ "/assets/images/fundamentals-in-formula/chassis-triangulation-diagram.png" | relative_url }})

This diagram shows off a part of the chassis in which “triangulation” is mandated. Basically, if you design your frame so that the bars meet at the same points, the frame will be much more resistant to bending or deforming. This makes things much safer in a crash, and, as it happens, performs much better as a racecar. 

The important takeaway here is that there are a lot of rules about how the car’s frame needs to be laid out in order to be safe. The exact particulars can be explored if/when you join your team’s Chassis sub-team.

### Vehicle Harness

Just as there’s a frame to protect the driver from crashes, there’s also a harness to hold the driver in. The harnesses allowed are either 5, 6, or 7 point harnesses, which means that the harnesses are designed to contact the body in 5, 6, or 7 places. They function like heavy-duty seatbelts and typically all of the straps meet in the middle of the driver’s torso in a center buckle. Here’s a picture of a 5-point harness:

### Brakes

The vehicle must have a braking system that is capable of locking all four wheels, meaning that when the car is moving, it is possible to push the brakes hard enough to lock each wheel so that you’d be sliding. This capability is tested on each car before it is certified to go out on track, so you will have to prove this in the competition. In racing, you normally do not want to lock your wheels when in braking, as it takes longer to slow down as compared to not locking up. It takes *more* braking force in order to lock your wheels into a slide than it does to simply slow them down. In the case of the rule, the inspectors need a way to determine if your braking system is *capable* of enough braking power to slow the vehicle. An easy proof is to show that the vehicle can lock its wheels while braking. For performance sake, you’ll want to avoid locking up the wheels during the actual performance events, but to prove that your car has powerful enough brakes, you’ll have to show off the ability to lock all four tires up.

*Rule T.3*

### Critical Fasteners

 “Fastener” is just a catch-all term for bolts and nuts, and some other types of joining things together. Most of the time, the term “fastener” means a bolt/nut combo. There are certain bolts on the vehicle that the organizers label as “Critical”, meaning they are extremely important for safety. These fasteners have some special rules applied to them, such as their quality grade. A quality grade on a fastener refers to how much force it can withstand before failing/fracturing/breaking. As you can imagine, the critical fasteners need to be of a high quality in order to guarantee the safety of the driver. Critical fasteners also need “Positive Locking Mechanisms”, which just means that they need a backup device/method to make sure that they cannot loosen. As you read deeper into the rules, you’ll notice that some bolts are called out as being a “Critical Fastener”, and now you know the basics on what that entails. 

*Rule T.8.2*

### Driver Gear & Details

The rules define what the driver must wear in order to be allowed to drive. Most of the driver gear is centered around flame-resistant material, because apart from the collision risks (which are mitigated by the frame rules & other rules), risk of fire is something that is present to the driver in the case of a crash. Even with the many rules about fire safety, fire is a very real possibility with the EV batteries that are used. I personally witnesses 2 battery fires at the competition, on two separate years. No one was hurt either occasion because of the fast action that the organizers took, but the risks were there. During operation, the driver is the closest one to the battery, and needs their gear to provide them the crucial time to distance themselves from the battery in the case of an emergency. This is what the driver clothing focuses on. Every inch of the driver will be covered in fire-resistant material when they are fully suited up. 

In addition, before the driver is given their wristband that signifies that they are approved to drive, they will need to demonstrate that they are capable of getting out of the car in under 5 seconds. This is called “driver egress”, and is tested for every driver on your team (minimum of 4 drivers per team). Egress is tested when the driver is fully suited up and fully buckled in - just as they would be when driving. The stopwatch starts when the driver first moves, and ends when they plant both feet outside of the vehicle. Egress will take some practice from your drivers.
